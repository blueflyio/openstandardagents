# Academia and Research Centers (Late 2025 to Early 2026)

Date of synthesis: 2026-04-05

## Cornell

eCornell's Agentic AI Architecture certificate (course code CISC13) frames a curriculum that moves from LLM fundamentals to RAG, multi-agent workflows, protocol-level interoperability (including MCP), and governance/risk/security practice. The sequencing indicates that institutions increasingly teach agent systems as socio-technical infrastructure, not only model prompting techniques. `[T33]`

## Harvard

Harvard Library Innovation Lab's Agent Protocols Tech Tree (published 2026-02-23) argues that open agent protocols function like early internet standards by making interoperability and ecosystem coordination explicit. The key academic contribution is not a single protocol proposal, but a shared map of protocol design space and community convergence points. `[T34]`

Harvard JOLT's 2026-02-28 policy analysis ("On the Institutional Origins of the Agentic Web") emphasizes governance externalities: platform conflicts, bot traffic growth, and the need for cryptographic identity, delegation boundaries, and auditability ("Know Your Agent" style controls). This pushes agent research beyond capability benchmarks toward institutional design. `[T42]`

## MIT

The MIT AI Agent Index 2025 (published 2026-02) documents 30 deployed agents and shows three important trends:
- rapid update cycles in 2024-2025,
- increasing autonomy and broader task scopes,
- safety/transparency disclosures lagging capability claims. `[T35][T36][T41]`

MIT Sloan's 2026 guidance for decision-makers states that agentic AI remains vulnerable to hallucinations and prompt injection in production settings, but forecasts large transaction-share growth by 2031. The research implication is that deployment momentum will continue even if safety maturity is incomplete. `[T37]`

## NIST and research-to-standards bridge

NIST NCCoE's 2026-02-05 concept paper on AI agent identity and authorization centers standards work around authentication, authorization, and lifecycle management for autonomous software agents. The associated NIST AI Agent Standards Initiative indicates an institution-level move from generic AI policy to agent-specific control requirements. `[T38][T39]`

## Peer-reviewed and preprint research

An interoperability survey (arXiv:2505.02279v2) compares MCP, ACP, A2A, and ANP and argues they should be adopted as a complementary stack with phased implementation. This is one of the clearest academic attempts to prevent protocol fragmentation by design. `[T40]`

The formal arXiv release of the AI Agent Index (arXiv:2602.17753) provides a structured technical/safety documentation method for deployed agents and is useful as a baseline measurement framework for future years. `[T41]`

## Academic consensus signals

Across Cornell, Harvard, MIT, and NIST-linked efforts, five recurring signals appear:

1. **Interoperability is foundational** (protocols as public infrastructure). `[T34][T40]`
2. **Identity and authorization are first-order research problems** for autonomous systems. `[T38][T39][T42]`
3. **Safety evidence trails deployment velocity** in current agent products. `[T35][T36][T37]`
4. **Education is shifting to full-stack agent architecture**, including governance and operations. `[T33]`
5. **Protocol and policy co-evolution is expected**, not sequential. `[T34][T42]`

## Evidence gaps and limitations

- Some university material is executive-education oriented and not peer-reviewed; those sources are used for curriculum and practice trends rather than scientific claims. `[T33][T37]`
- Several ecosystem metrics are snapshot-based and may change quickly due to weekly releases and repository growth.
- Access to some institutional outputs (for example full reports behind forms or events) is limited; where possible, official summaries and companion documents were used instead.
