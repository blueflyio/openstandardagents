# Academia: University, Research-Center, and Scholarly Signals (2025-2026)

Date: 2026-04-08

## 1) Cornell (eCornell): curriculum-level production framing

Cornell’s Agentic AI Architecture certificate explicitly sequences:

1. LLM/prompt foundations,
2. RAG/context engineering,
3. agentic architecture and protocol use (including MCP),
4. governance/risk/security/human oversight.

This is notable because it treats governance and oversight as core competency rather than post-deployment compliance add-on [R22].

### Practical insight

Cornell’s sequencing mirrors production reality:

- first establish reliable model behavior,
- then grounding/context,
- then autonomous tool use,
- finally policy/governance hardening.

This order is useful for enterprise enablement roadmaps.

## 2) Harvard ecosystem: protocol governance as institutional architecture

Harvard Library Innovation Lab’s Agent Protocols Tech Tree argues open protocols are an “x-ray” into what builders are truly converging on, analogous to early web protocol formation [R23].

Core contributions:

- technical map of protocol layers for mixed audiences,
- framing of agent protocols as governance levers,
- explicit argument that agents are difficult to regulate only via centralized control due to low barriers to agent construction [R23][R24].

Related Harvard policy/legal commentary extends this into institutional questions:

- who governs the “agentic web,”
- platform-control versus protocol-control tradeoffs,
- identity/delegation/accountability requirements for scalable autonomous activity [R30].

### Practical insight

Harvard’s work is strongest as a **governance framing layer**:
protocol choices determine feasible behavior and accountability surfaces.

## 3) MIT: empirical index + executive operating guidance

MIT AI Agent Index (2025 edition) provides one of the most structured public snapshots:

- 30 prominent agents examined,
- 24/30 with launches/major updates in 2024-2025,
- major safety transparency gaps (4/13 frontier-autonomy agents disclosing safety evaluations),
- no established standards for web conduct among agents,
- concentration around GPT/Claude/Gemini families [R25].

MIT Sloan guidance (2026 decision-maker actions) adds an operations perspective:

- agentic AI “not ready for prime time” in many contexts due to hallucination and prompt-injection risk,
- expectation that meaningful transaction coverage may still scale quickly over coming years,
- recommendation to build internal capacity while preserving human-in-the-loop controls [R26].

MIT SMR’s “nine essential questions” reinforces that the difficult edge is cross-system action security, attribution, and lifecycle accountability [R27].

### Practical insight

MIT’s combined message:

- adoption is real,
- capability is growing quickly,
- governance and transparency maturity are lagging.

## 4) Broader scholarship (arXiv and standards-adjacent literature)

### Interoperability protocol survey

A 2025 survey compares MCP, ACP, A2A, and ANP and proposes phased adoption patterns based on use case [R28].

### TRiSM for agentic multi-agent systems

TRiSM review work frames trust/risk/security in multi-agent LLM systems with emphasis on explainability, ModelOps, security/privacy, and lifecycle governance [R29].

### W3C community white-paper stream

ANP/W3C community material emphasizes transition from GUI-centric interactions to protocol-native agent interactions, with identity/discovery/data-flow concerns as first-order protocol requirements [R19].

## 5) Evidence quality and limitations

- University pages and institute blogs are strong for agenda-setting but not always empirical.
- MIT AI Agent Index offers stronger comparative structure than most public sources, but is still bounded by publicly available disclosures [R25].
- Some policy pieces (student review, legal commentary) are directional framing, not controlled measurement [R30][R31].

## 6) Applied takeaway for R&D and governance teams

For an enterprise program, a research-aligned maturity ladder is:

1. Model/task reliability baseline,
2. Context/RAG quality and observability,
3. Agent/tool orchestration with explicit safety envelopes,
4. Protocol interoperability strategy,
5. Identity/authorization/audit controls at runtime.

This ladder is consistent with Cornell curriculum design, MIT risk findings, and NIST/NCCoE priorities [R22][R25][R41].
